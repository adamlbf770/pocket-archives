# Pocket Archives: A Documentary History of Pokémon

> Research dossier, first documentary edition — 16 August 2026. Accuracy takes priority over completeness. Claims are classified as **verified**, **probable**, **disputed**, or **unresolved**. Dates refer to the historical object or event—not the date of a modern scan or reconstruction.

## Research method

Each significant claim should preserve five distinctions:

1. **Historical claim** — what is alleged to have happened.
2. **Evidence** — the document, object, or participant statement supporting it.
3. **Source class** — contemporary primary, retrospective participant testimony, official corporate history, archival research, or later secondary account.
4. **Conflict/limitation** — what the source cannot establish or where another source disagrees.
5. **Confidence** — verified, probable, disputed, or unresolved.

Image records separately identify the original creator, historical object, publication context, current digital source, and rights status.

The working image ledger is maintained in [`image-provenance-register.csv`](./image-provenance-register.csv). A web-visible image is not automatically reusable: copyright in the depicted art, ownership of the physical object, and copyright in the photograph or scan are separate questions.

## Executive history

Pokémon did not begin as a completed fictional universe or as the direct transcription of one childhood memory. Satoshi Tajiri's insect collecting is genuine autobiography, but the surviving record places it beside other formative practices: observing systems, mastering arcade games, publishing obscure information, building a network of collaborators through the *Game Freak* mini-comic, and wanting electronic play to carry something meaningful between people. The childhood story explains part of the sensibility. The fanzine and arcade culture explain how that sensibility became method.

The decisive game idea was exchange. Tajiri saw in the Game Boy link cable a channel that could move a valued creature rather than merely synchronize competition. The 1990 *Capsule Monsters* proposal gave that idea a world, visible creatures, capture imagery, battle scenes, facilities, and routes. Yet the proposal was not a frozen blueprint. Its publicly disclosed pages appear to collect ideas at different stages, and later staff testimony says major goals—including explicit Pokédex completion—solidified during development. Treating every page as a single 1990 moment falsifies the evidence's layered character.

Development took roughly six elapsed years but, by Tajiri's near-contemporary account, about four active years. Game Freak interrupted the project to complete paid software, including Nintendo work that both financed the company and taught its small staff how to ship commercial games. Hardware pressure was creative pressure: a 512 KB cartridge, monochrome sprites, constrained names, and contested bytes required creatures to read as compact symbols. The roster was collaborative. Programmers, planners, and artists proposed ideas; Sugimori directed, revised, and unified them into a coherent public visual language. “Sugimori drew the official art” is therefore important and true where documented; “Sugimori alone designed all 151” is not.

*Pocket Monsters Red and Green* arrived on 27 February 1996 in an unfavorable sales window for aging hardware. The launch was not a day-one global-style explosion. Growth accumulated through paired versions, trading, strategy information, manga, and especially the social mystery of Mew. *CoroCoro Comic* transformed an unobtainable 151st creature into a scarce transferable prize: twenty initial recipients drew about 78,000 applications. Mew did not single-handedly create the success, but it revealed the franchise's central engine—information, scarcity, software, print, and child-to-child explanation reinforcing one another.

Cards expanded that engine in two different directions. Bandai Carddass turned the roster and game data into inexpensive vending-machine collectibles by late September 1996; an earlier oversize Chip Shooter product remains a credible card-like predecessor under primary-source review. The Creatures-designed TCG, released at retail on 20 October after two *CoroCoro* promos, translated the game's six-creature team, types, attacks, and collecting logic into an independent paper game. It also printed artist credits directly on mass objects and opened Pokémon's visual world to multiple illustrators. Later controversy over Akabane-provenanced “prototype” cards demonstrates why a design's historical authenticity and a physical print's production date must be proved separately.

Television made the system legible without a Game Boy. The anime could not give Satoshi/Ash one of three player-selected starters without privileging a choice, so its producers elevated Pikachu—already internally liked, but not yet the inevitable mascot. Voice, movement, repetition, and companionship turned one creature into the international emblem, and animation then fed back into later Pikachu drawing. The 16 December 1997 photosensitivity incident interrupted production and changed broadcast-safety practice; it should be remembered precisely, without collapsing 685 hospital transports into 685 identical epilepsy diagnoses.

By 1997–98, Pokémon had become an environment in Japan. A child could stamp a railway booklet, open a bread sticker, buy a ¥20 vending card, use a Pokémon notebook, see an ANA cabin transformed into a branded space, or enter Pokémon Center Tokyo. These ordinary and disposable objects are central historical evidence because they show how a fictional ecology occupied daily routes. The most informative archive is therefore not only cartridges and trophy cards, but wrappers, tickets, inserts, bags, receipts, displays, and used binders that retain how the system was encountered.

International expansion was staged rather than instantaneous. In the United States, television introduced the characters before Red and Blue, and the TCG followed; names, slogans, packaging, and broadcast content changed, while the creatures' fundamental designs did not. Britain, continental Europe, and Australia followed related but nonidentical sequences, with different broadcaster, shop, and product dates. “Pokémon launched in Europe” is not one event. It is a set of local clocks that must be reconstructed from network schedules, retailer evidence, games, cards, promotions, and contemporary reactions.

Collecting likewise changed form. The first collectors completed, traded, played, and used objects. Internet markets, price guides, grading, population reports, and registries later elevated condition and comparison. The 2016 nostalgia wave and the 2020–21 pandemic/influencer boom accelerated financialization; record trophy sales then made a tiny apex look like the whole field. Pocket Archives should preserve a different hierarchy: provenance, historical function, context, and explanatory power first; rarity, grade, and price as separate attributes.

## Part I — Before Pokémon

### The insect-collecting account: evidence problem

The earliest direct first-person account located in this audit so far is TIME's 1999 interview with Tajiri. Tajiri described the rice paddies, rivers, and forests of childhood Machida; his fascination with finding and observing insects; and the rapid conversion of those places into developed land and arcades. The interview establishes that insect collecting was a genuine autobiographical influence. It does **not** establish that this was the sole or immediate origin of Pokémon. In the same interview Tajiri also discussed *Ultraman*, arcade games, the *Game Freak* magazine network, and dissatisfaction with existing games. [TIME interview transcript preserved by PokeDream](https://pokedream.com/pokemon/infocenter/tajiri.php); [TIME cover story, 22 Nov. 1999](https://content.time.com/time/subscriber/article/0,33009,992625-4,00.html)

**Source-genealogy finding:** “Tajiri collected insects” is directly supported by Tajiri's testimony by 1999. The stronger popular formulation—“he invented Pokémon specifically to recreate childhood bug collecting”—is a later causal compression unless an earlier, more explicit first-person statement is found.

### Game Freak: publication before company

A 2013 Japanese interview with Ken Sugimori describes Game Freak as originating in a mini-comic that anticipated aspects of game magazines and strategy guides, then moving into self-developed software with *Quinty*, which was pitched to Namco. This is valuable retrospective participant testimony, but it must be paired with surviving issues, colophons, and contemporary records before exact dates and operating practices are stated as settled facts. [4Gamer interview with Ken Sugimori and Tetsuya Watanabe, 8 Oct. 2013](https://www.4gamer.net/games/226/G022607/20131007064/)

Tajiri's 1999 TIME interview adds operational detail: the publication began as handwritten, stapled pages carrying strategies and secrets; Tajiri said a special issue on *Xevious* sold approximately 10,000 copies at ¥300 and that increased circulation led him from photocopying to commercial printing. This is first-person testimony, but sales quantity and issue chronology still require corroboration from surviving copies or publishing records. [TIME interview transcript](https://pokedream.com/pokemon/infocenter/tajiri.php)

## Part II — Capsule Monsters

### 1989 versus 1990

The strongest surviving object-level evidence currently supports **1990** for the extant proposal pages: the cover and Kanto layout map carry that date, and the cover identifies Satoshi Tajiri as planner/producer. This does not rule out an earlier origin for the underlying idea. It only establishes that the shown document existed in a 1990 form. Claims of a specific 1989 conception date require an independent contemporary document or a clearly dated participant statement. [Helix Chamber, “Capsule Monsters—Cover and Pitch”](https://helixchamber.com/media/capsule-monsters/capsulemonsters_01/); [Helix Chamber Capsule Monsters archive](https://helixchamber.com/media/capsule-monsters/)

The item-level working census is maintained in [`capsule-monsters-artifact-register.csv`](./capsule-monsters-artifact-register.csv). It currently records twenty-one disclosed pages, sheets, or modern disclosure records with separate fields for historical object, current scan, authorship, confidence, and display rights. This is a **publicly located corpus**, not proof that only these objects survive; Helix Chamber reports that the underlying booklet contained roughly one hundred pages and has never been published in full.

## Part III — The long development, 1990–1996

### Link cable and trading

In the July 2000 Nintendo Online Magazine staff interview, Sugimori said the initial concept was explained through the Game Boy link cable and analogies to exchanging *menko* or trading cards. The team understood the exchange idea before it understood how to make that idea into a game. This is retrospective participant testimony published by Nintendo, not a surviving 1990 requirements document; it nevertheless closely matches the visible trading emphasis in the proposal material. [English translation preserved by Dogasu](https://dogasu.bulbagarden.net/features/books_magazines/pokemon_in_1996_developers_interview.html); [alternate translation by GlitterBerri](https://glitterberri.com/game-freak-staff-interview/creating-the-game/)

### A collaborative creature-design process

The same interview directly contradicts the myth of a single designer producing the roster. Sugimori described a ten-person staff in which programmers, graphics staff, planners, and others contributed character ideas outside their formal specialties. He further recalled that roughly one and a half times the final number of creatures had been designed. The exact set represented by “1.5 times” is not enumerated, so it cannot by itself authenticate any particular leaked or reconstructed creature. [Nintendo Online Magazine interview translation](https://dogasu.bulbagarden.net/features/books_magazines/pokemon_in_1996_developers_interview.html)

### Pokédex completion emerged during development

Sugimori recalled that the project began as a more conventional RPG and only around the middle of development adopted completion of the Pokémon bestiary as an explicit goal. Memory limits then constrained roster size. This account is evidence against narratives that present all final systems as fully formed at the original pitch. [GlitterBerri translation of the July 2000 staff interview](https://glitterberri.com/game-freak-staff-interview/creating-the-game/)

### Six elapsed years, about four active years

The phrase “six years in development” is literally true as elapsed time but misleading if read as six years of uninterrupted production. In a staff interview published in the first year of the franchise, Tajiri said work had taken place mainly during the first year and the final three years; between those phases, Game Freak stopped or sharply reduced Pokémon work to complete other games. This early testimony is unusually valuable because it predates the franchise's later corporate mythology. [Modern translation of the 1996 *Pocket Monsters Illustrated Book* interview](https://lavacutcontent.com/satoshi-tajiri-pokedex-interview/)

The July 2000 Nintendo Online Magazine interview supplies the economic logic. Tajiri recalled initially believing that a compact, non-action Game Boy game could be made in roughly six months. As the scope expanded, Ishihara advised Game Freak to undertake smaller commissioned projects—including *Yoshi no Tamago* and *Mario & Wario*—to bring money into the company and build the staff before returning its focus to Pokémon. Tajiri explicitly characterized the work as financially necessary and creatively useful rather than an abandonment of the project. [Tajiri–Ishihara interview translation](https://lavacutcontent.com/satoshi-tajiri-ishihara-interview/)

**Best current interpretation:** 1990–1996 is the project's incubation span. Approximately four of those years were active production by Tajiri's 1996 recollection. Work for Nintendo both sustained Game Freak and taught the team how to finish tightly specified commercial games. Precise start/stop months, contracts, payments, and the formal roles of Ape and Creatures remain unresolved.

### Cartridge limits shaped form, not merely content

Masuda's 2018 account describes a 512 KB program assembled under extreme space pressure. Late debugging sometimes traded execution speed for one-byte-shorter instructions so the recovered bytes could hold fixes. Masuda programmed parts of the game as well as the sound system, music, cries, and effects; he produced the cries by distorting PSG waveforms. He also connected the Japanese five-character name limits to the technical constraints. [Denfaminicogamer interview, section “『ポケモン』初代を生み出した職人芸”](https://news.denfaminicogamer.jp/interview/180608/3)

A 2014 music interview independently supports the sound account. Masuda said he handled the overall program, the music-playback program, all music, cries, and effects. Space was sufficiently contested that one additional song could be weighed against one additional Pokémon; repetition and avoiding rests saved kilobytes. [2083WEB interview, “すべてのはじまり、『ポケットモンスター 赤・緑』の楽曲に迫る”](https://www.2083.jp/contents/201410gamefreak/page_01.html)

The visual design was subject to the same economy. Sugimori described sprites as simplified symbols of more complex creatures: the small monochrome screen and limited pixel budget required immediately legible silhouettes. The result was not simply “retro style,” but a production system in which technical compression and character readability reinforced one another. [July 2000 Nintendo Online Magazine staff interview, modern translation](https://lavacutcontent.com/sugimori-masuda-developer-interview/)

### Late completion and a poor-looking release window

Ishihara recalled that the games were substantially complete in October 1995, too late for the year-end season, and therefore shipped at the end of February 1996—a weak retail period—when the Game Boy itself appeared to be declining. This explains the release's modest tone without proving that Nintendo lacked confidence in the software: Ishihara said he considered it exceptional while remaining worried about its timing. [Nintendo, *Iwata Asks: Pokémon HeartGold and SoulSilver*, “Just Making the Last Train”](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/ds/pokemon/0/0/)

Research continuing: contract chronology; Ape/Creatures and Ishihara's role; precise interruptions; staffing by year; backup-memory decision; capture, storage, evolution, map, and interface milestones.

## Part IV — Creature design and authorship

No blanket “Sugimori designed all 151” attribution will be used. The authorship table will record only Pokémon for which a designer attribution can be traced to a participant interview, design document, exhibition label, official publication, or equivalent evidence.

The team itself resisted simple creator credits. In a 2018 official interview, Sugimori explained that multiple people proposed and modified ideas and that Game Freak had therefore generally avoided saying one person “designed” a particular Pokémon unless it could tell the full collaborative story. The table below distinguishes initial designer, substantial revision, art direction, sprite production, and final official illustration whenever the evidence permits. [The Pokémon Company press release and creator interview](https://prtimes.jp/main/html/rd/p/000000026.000026665.html)

| Pokémon | Supported role | Evidence | Original source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pikachu | Atsuko Nishida: initial design and name; Ken Sugimori: art direction/final official art; Koji Nishino: repeated “cuter” revisions and encounter design | Nishida described an Electric-type, originally two-evolution assignment; all three participants described the revision process. | 2018 official “Creators of Pikachu” interview, preserved in Japanese archives and English translation | High |
| Raichu | Atsuko Nishida: initial design | Nishida said she designed Pikachu and Raichu from the assignment. | 2018 official creator interview | High |
| Gorochu (unused) | Atsuko Nishida: initial design | Nishida described the fanged, horned third stage; Nishino and Sugimori said it was cut for game-balance/data reasons, not visual rejection. | 2018 official creator interview | High for testimony; no authenticated public design image located |
| Clefairy | Ken Sugimori: design | Sugimori explicitly referred to “the Clefairy I designed.” | 2018 official creator interview | High |
| Mew | Shigeki Morimoto: character creation/program insertion; other visual-finalization roles pending | The July 2000 official staff feature identifies Morimoto as Mew's creator and describes its insertion after debugging freed space. | Nintendo Online Magazine staff feature; translations under comparison | High for Morimoto's creation role; implementation details require Japanese-text audit |

**Important correction:** Sugimori produced or supervised the coherent official illustration set, but “drew the official picture,” “directed the design,” and “originated the creature” are different credits. The dossier will not infer one from another.

## Part V — Ken Sugimori

Sugimori's historical importance is best understood as a chain of roles, not as the slogan “designer of all 151.” He entered the Game Freak network through its mini-comic phase, supplied illustration and page-making skills to the publication, worked on the independently developed *Quinty*, illustrated the *Capsule Monsters* proposal, and became the visual editor through whom many different staff ideas acquired a coherent public form. In 2013 he described Game Freak's origin in the mini-comic and the move from an amateur circle to a developer through *Quinty* and Namco; the interview identifies him as having participated since the publication and as Game Freak's art director. [4Gamer interview, 8 Oct. 2013](https://www.4gamer.net/games/226/G022607/20131007064/)

His most defensible authorship claim is therefore **visual unification**. In Nintendo's 2010 interview, Sugimori said that designers proposed creatures collectively, that he judged whether they belonged in the Pokémon world, drew the final official illustrations, and produced multi-angle drawings before pixel work so inconsistencies could be resolved. That later-generation description cannot be projected mechanically onto every 1990–96 asset, but it corroborates the collaborative process described by the original team in 2000. [Nintendo, *Iwata Asks: Pokémon Black and White*, “A Completely New Pokémon World”](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/ds/pokemon-black-white/0/1/)

The familiar early illustration set should be cataloged as a publication system:

| Visual layer | Historical function | Attribution rule |
|---|---|---|
| Development sketch | Tests silhouette, anatomy, mechanics, or personality | Credit the named drafter only when a document or participant source identifies one |
| Sprite | Operational in-game image constrained by Game Boy memory and screen | Do not infer sprite artist from the later official illustration |
| Model/turnaround | Reconciles views for sprite, animation, merchandise, or later production | Record preparer and reviser separately where known |
| Official character illustration | Creates a stable, reusable public identity for manuals, guides, packaging, and licensing | Credit Sugimori where the original publication or participant testimony supports it; do not convert that credit into sole creature conception |
| Card-specific illustration | A composition made for a particular Carddass or TCG object | Read the printed card credit; distinguish commissioned art from reused stock art |

The pale washes, visible linework, and irregular pigments of the early official images are part of their historical meaning, but “watercolor Sugimori art” has become a loose online label. The archive must preserve the first printed appearance and highest-quality authorized reproduction for each image. Later digital files may be color-shifted, cleaned, composited, or reconstructed. A visually attractive scan is not necessarily a faithful record of the original sheet.

Research still required: a physical census of *Game Freak* mini-comic issues and colophons; page-level credits in *Quinty* and the 1990 proposal; original-media confirmation for each early illustration; the first publication of the 151-character official set; and a card-by-card separation of stock art from art commissioned for Bandai Carddass and the TCG.

## Part VI — 1996 launch

### A quiet release, then an expanding social mystery

Nintendo's official product records fix the Japanese release at 27 February 1996 and document Red and Green as distinct versions with different encounter rates and version-exclusive Pokémon. This is the commercial implementation of the social design: no single cartridge could produce every normal encounter. [Pokémon official Red/Green product page](https://www.pokemon.co.jp/game/other/gb-rg/)

Later reports repeatedly give an opening shipment of roughly 230,000 units, but they do not always distinguish first-day, first-week, and initial allocation. A 2026 Dengeki retrospective explicitly separates approximately 130,000 on day one from approximately 230,000 during the first week. Until a 1996 shipment notice or trade chart is entered, this dossier treats **230,000 as a probable early-week shipment figure**, not an unquestionable “first shipment.”

The clearest documented inflection point is *CoroCoro Comic*. Its official archive retrospective states that the magazine first covered Pokémon in its March 1996 issue; its May issue offered the hidden 151st Pokémon, Mew, to twenty readers and received about 78,000 applications; an additional hundred-recipient offer followed; and its November issue began Kosaku Anakubo's manga while also offering the Blue version through exclusive mail order. [CoroCoro Online, 1996 archive retrospective](https://corocoro-news.jp/news/49271/)

This chronology supports a restrained claim: Mew gave the cartridge's invisible social layer a concrete, scarce object, while CoroCoro supplied mass communication and physical distribution. It does **not** prove that Mew alone caused the sales curve. Manga, strategy coverage, trading between children, two-version design, repeated distribution events, and later anime exposure are separable mechanisms.

### Mew: secret, insertion, and distribution must be separated

Three events are often collapsed:

1. **Creation and insertion:** Morimoto created Mew and placed its data into the program after debugging freed space, according to the July 2000 staff feature.
2. **Rumor and discovery:** players encountered hints, glitches, or reports of a 151st creature; the exact earliest public rumor chain requires contemporary magazine and playground evidence.
3. **Authorized distribution:** the May 1996 *CoroCoro* campaign formally offered Mew data to twenty cartridge owners and generated approximately 78,000 applications.

The promotional result is well supported by CoroCoro's own archive. Details such as individual trainer IDs and the precise transfer procedure are currently supported by event-preservation research and must be tied to the original 1996 instructions before being stated as fully verified.

Research continuing: digitize the first *CoroCoro* article and Mew offer pages; reconcile shipment versus sell-through figures; locate the one-million campaign advertisement; trace Blue's application and fulfillment dates.

## Part VII — Cards: TCG, Carddass, and prototypes

### “First card” is not one question

Pokémon appeared on several card-like objects in 1996, and later collectors have repeatedly collapsed them into one race for “first.” A defensible chronology must separate at least five categories:

| Category | Earliest currently supported date | Function | Historical status |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Development proof/playtest | 1995–96 designs; surviving physical print dates disputed item by item | Internal game design and presentation | Historically important designs, but the large 2024 market release contains questioned and apparently modern-printed examples |
| Jumbo Carddass “Chip Shooter” | Late June 1996 claim under primary-source review | Oversize peelable card/coin play product | A credible candidate for earliest mass-issued Pokémon card-like product; not a standard pocket card or the TCG |
| Bandai Carddass | Late Sept. 1996 | Vending-machine character/image cards, not the Creatures TCG | Probably the first mass-retail Pokémon card series; exact day not yet proved |
| CoroCoro TCG promos | 15 Oct. 1996 issue date | Playable Pikachu/Jigglypuff promotional cards | Likely the first publicly issued cards compatible with the Creatures TCG |
| Pocket Monsters Card Game starter/expansion | 20 Oct. 1996 | Full commercial trading card game | First retail release of the playable TCG |
| Topsun gum cards | Evidence presently favors 1997, despite a 1995 copyright line | Gum-insert collectible cards | Commonly mislabeled “1995”; copyright year is not a manufacturing date |

The official Pokémon Card Game chronicle fixes the first starter and expansion pack to **20 October 1996**. Creatures states that it has handled planning, development, and production of the game continuously since 1996. These are strong corporate records for the retail TCG date and organizational lineage. [Pokémon Card Game 25th anniversary chronicle](https://www.pokemon-card.com/ex/25th/chronicle/); [Creatures card-game business history](https://www.creatures.co.jp/service/cardgame)

The first Bandai Carddass series appears in contemporary “September new product” listings with late-September sale language, and the second part was advertised for early October. This currently supports “late September 1996,” not a single exact date. A surviving Bandai order sheet, distributor invoice, machine-placement record, or dated receipt would improve the claim. Collector chronologies that assert “23 September” without such an object are not sufficient.

### Carddass as a separate card culture

The best presently assembled primary trail is *Carddass News* issue 51 plus the October 1996 *CoroCoro Comic* advertisement on page 154. The latter says Part 1 would go on sale in **late September** (9月下旬) and Part 2 around **early October** (10月上旬). Those Japanese phrases identify thirds of a month, not exact days; converting them into 21–30 September and 1–10 October is a calendrical explanation, not evidence for a particular street date. [collector reconstruction with scans and transcriptions](https://www.elitefourum.com/t/1996-verified-timeline-of-pokemon-card-release-dates/37138)

Parts 1 and 2 were sold through Bandai's Carddass vending system at ¥20 for one card or ¥100 for five. They were slightly smaller than later TCG cards and functioned as character/Pokédex data cards rather than a playable battle game. Their fronts mainly circulated the unified official character art; their backs connected the cards to Red/Green through sprites, encounter information, size data, and occasional additional illustrations. The system turned game knowledge into something collectible at a vending machine.

Parts 3 and 4, advertised for mid-April and early June 1997 respectively, changed the visual proposition: the 151-character sequence used dramatic action compositions and signature moves, with special/prism cards and associated jumbo material. Modern checklists describe the art as unique to this Carddass program, but blanket artist attribution still needs the original Bandai credit sheet or a participant statement. A 1996 *Carddass News* notice about dozens of newly drawn images is evidence of commissioning, not by itself proof that one artist executed every piece.

**Acquisition priority:** obtain the Part 1 and Part 2 vending headers, *Carddass News* issue 51, October 1996 *CoroCoro* page 154, an unopened vending box or intact five-card band, and the Part 3/4 Pikachu jumbo carrying the later schedule. Together these objects establish manufacture, price, distribution, chronology, and visual change more securely than a row of high-grade singles.

The reported late-June 1996 Jumbo Carddass “Chip Shooter” product could move the earliest public card-like object back several months. Because the current trace runs through collector research and a draft reference page, it remains **probable, not closed** until its contemporary advertisement, package, or Bandai record is cataloged. It should be described separately from both standard Carddass and the TCG.

### What the card game changed

The TCG did more than reproduce the video game. It converted software attributes into paper rules, made every creature a portable illustration, attached an artist credit to the object, and created randomized acquisition outside a Game Boy. The original set mixed Sugimori's unified stock art with commissioned images by Mitsuhiro Arita, Keiji Kinebuchi, Ken Sugimori, and others; a card-by-card art and credit census will be built from physical Japanese examples rather than English reprints.

Ishihara is consistently identified as the initiating producer. A 2019 Creatures interview reports secondhand that, while Red/Green were still being developed, he recognized that a roster of collectible creatures and turn-based battles would translate naturally to a trading card game. Because the speaker joined later, this is institutional memory, not eyewitness testimony. The original design credits commonly name Ishihara, Kōichi Ōyama, Takumi Akabane, and sometimes Akihiko Miura; those credits require verification against 1996 rulebooks and card packaging before the final people map is closed. [Famitsu interview with Creatures card developers](https://www.famitsu.com/news/201903/14173198.html)

### The Akabane prototype controversy

Hundreds of alleged proof-of-concept, prototype, playtest, and presentation pieces entered the market in 2023–24 with provenance attributed to former Creatures developer Takumi Akabane. Grading and auction companies initially presented portions of the material as authentic 1995–96 artifacts. In January 2025, collectors reported printer tracking marks inconsistent with the claimed production dates. CGC then publicly instructed that transactions in a specified list of its holders stop and requested their return for investigation. [CGC statement, 31 Jan. 2025, updated 4 Feb.](https://www.cgccards.com/news/article/13730/)

The official notice currently enumerates **813 certification numbers**. As checked on 16 August 2026, that page still describes an investigation, says transactions should be halted, and offers holder review; it does **not** publish an item-by-item conclusion or use “Not Genuine” for the listed certificates. Pocket Archives preserves the exact official list in [`cgc-impacted-certificates.csv`](./cgc-impacted-certificates.csv), generated reproducibly by [`update-cgc-certificate-register.py`](./update-cgc-certificate-register.py). A changed online lookup, auction withdrawal, or collector screenshot may be useful evidence for an individual object, but none substitutes for a dated CGC determination or a forensic report.

This creates three distinct historical questions:

1. Are the **graphic designs** faithful to actual 1995–96 playtest stages?
2. Were the **physical pieces** offered in 2024 actually printed in 1995–96?
3. What is the provenance of each individual object from creation to consignment?

Evidence that a design existed in the 1990s does not authenticate a newly printed physical manifestation. Conversely, proof that an offered sheet was printed recently does not by itself prove that its digital layout was invented recently. Until CGC publishes item-level results and primary Creatures records are available, the entire market group must be treated as **disputed**, with no acquisition recommendation based only on a slab label or an Akabane attribution.

## Part VIII — Anime and cultural expansion

### Why the partner was Pikachu

The anime began in Japan on 1 April 1997. Its central adaptation problem was structural: the game asks each player to choose Bulbasaur, Charmander, or Squirtle, but a fixed television protagonist could not choose one without alienating viewers who chose another. Director Kunihiko Yuyama later explained that the team therefore selected a Pokémon unavailable as a starting choice; Pikachu also offered visual action through Electric attacks and was neither strongly coded male nor female. [Yuyama interview preserved by Trance Cell](https://trance-cell.com/interview-youc/); [Masakazu Kubo retrospective interview](https://www.1101.com/n/s/14editors/masakazu_kubo/2021-10-28.html)

This was an adaptation decision, not evidence that Pikachu was designed from the beginning to be the franchise mascot. The 2018 creator interview says Pikachu won an internal visual survey and received one of fifteen manual illustrations, but Sugimori did not know how players would respond. Only after the game, television casting, Ikue Ōtani's voice, and repeated merchandise did Pikachu become the singular global emblem.

### The anime changed the creature designs in return

Sugimori stated that animation's need for expressive movement influenced later Pikachu art: the initially short, stout body developed a clearer neck and longer spine to act more easily. This is a documented case of reverse adaptation—the television model did not merely copy game art; it fed back into subsequent game-era character depiction. [2018 official Pikachu creator interview, preserved English version](https://nintendobserver.com/2018/08/pokemon-creator-profile-the-creators-of-pikachu/)

Series organizer Takeshi Shudō's long retrospective columns are a valuable first-person record of the writing room, but they are memoir, not production minutes. They show that the writers framed Ash–Pikachu companionship, childhood autonomy, and a “Stand by Me” coming-of-age sensibility as central themes. They must be checked against dated proposals, scripts, and credit sheets before attributing every concept to Shudō alone. [Shudō's Anime Style columns](https://www.style.fm/as/05_column/shudo201.shtml)

### The December 1997 broadcast crisis

On 16 December 1997, episode 38 used a short field of rapidly alternating red and blue light. In testimony reported by *The Japan Times* eight days later, TV Tokyo president Yutaka Ichiki told a Diet committee that an estimated 4.15 million households had been watching and that the Fire Defense Agency counted **685 people transported to hospitals**; two remained hospitalized. The article distinguishes “rushed to hospitals” from a diagnosis of epilepsy. [*The Japan Times*, 24 Dec. 1997](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/1997/12/24/national/tv-tokyo-apologizes-to-diet-over-injurious-animation/)

That distinction matters. Later headlines often say “685 seizures,” but subsequent medical work found a mixture of true seizures, visually induced illness, migraine-like symptoms, and other reactions. A five-year follow-up obtained data for 91 of 103 treated patients in its sample and separated 24 with a prior epilepsy history from 67 without one. The incident was medically real; the popular single-number diagnosis is too blunt. [Takahashi and Tsukahara, *New England Journal of Medicine*, 22 July 2004](https://www.nejm.org/doi/abs/10.1056/NEJM200407223510424)

TVQ's institutional history records the emergency response and identifies the rapid color-flashing technique known as *paka-paka* as the central production hazard. The broadcast industry subsequently adopted animation-image guidelines in 1998. This episode belongs in the history not as sensational folklore but as a turning point in audiovisual safety, broadcast governance, and the franchise's global reputation. The archive should preserve a contemporary newspaper, network apology/report, guideline document, and safely represented still frames—not autoplay the hazardous sequence. [TVQ 35th-anniversary history](https://www.tvq.co.jp/history35th/modal/h1997-09.html)

Research continuing: initial proposal and sponsor structure; OLM/ShoPro/TV Tokyo roles; first-episode production; Team Rocket; the 16 December 1997 broadcast incident and hiatus; film greenlight; regional localization edits.

## Part IX — International Pokémania

### The United States was a coordinated sequence, not a single launch

Japan's order was game (Feb. 1996), retail TCG (Oct. 1996), then television (Apr. 1997). Ishihara later described the American strategy as deliberately different: television first to teach the characters, then the video game, then the card game. The localized anime entered US syndication on 7 September 1998; Red and Blue followed later that month; the English TCG's formal Base Set release followed in January 1999. [Ishihara interview in Famitsu, 2023](https://www.famitsu.com/news/202310/19320055.html); [contemporary US trade report on the anime and Hasbro program](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/monster-in-hasbros-pocket/1100-2463848/)

The order mattered. Television taught names, personalities, and relationships without requiring hardware; the paired cartridges then turned that knowledge into personal collection and exchange; cards made it visible at school and retail. “Pokémon arrived in America in September 1998” is therefore shorthand for a staged media campaign whose components had different licensees, dates, and functions.

### Localization without replacing the creatures

Ishihara recalled that some US staff judged the creatures too cute and supplied dramatically altered proposals. He rejected redesigning the cast, while Minoru Arakawa's later account similarly says Nintendo of America ultimately kept the original designs. Because the often-repeated “tiger with breasts” line comes from a translated retrospective and the drawings remain unpublished, the exact appearance of the proposal cannot be independently evaluated. What is secure is the outcome: the international launch kept the recognizable Japanese creature designs while changing names, text, packaging, slogans, and broadcast content. [July 2000 Tajiri–Ishihara interview translation](https://lavacutcontent.com/satoshi-tajiri-ishihara-interview/)

Iwata's role was technical as well as managerial. In Nintendo's 2009 retrospective, he and Morimoto described how Iwata analyzed the poorly documented Red/Green source, helped define the localization approach, reproduced the battle logic for Nintendo 64 work, and created graphic-compression tools while Game Freak continued Gold/Silver. This supports a more precise account than the myth that he simply “compressed Gold and Silver so Kanto would fit.” [Nintendo, *Iwata Asks*, “Just Being President Was A Waste!”](https://www.nintendo.com/en-gb/Iwata-Asks/Iwata-Asks-Pokemon-HeartGold-Version-SoulSilver-Version/Iwata-Asks-Pokemon-HeartGold-Version-SoulSilver-Version/3-Just-Being-President-Was-A-Waste-/3-Just-Being-President-Was-A-Waste--225951.html)

### Europe and Australia were not delayed copies of the United States

The rollout outside Japan and North America must be reconstructed territory by territory. Nintendo's European history says Pokémon was released “throughout Europe” on 8 October 1999, but contemporary British reporting describes a Westminster launch on 30 September and product entering UK shops on 1 October. These statements can coexist if the corporate date refers to a wider regional release rather than every national shelf date. The dossier should not erase the difference by assigning a single date to “Europe.” [Nintendo Europe corporate history](https://www.nintendo.com/en-gb/Hardware/Nintendo-History/Nintendo-History-625945.html); [*The Guardian*, 1 Oct. 1999](https://www.theguardian.com/business/1999/oct/01/8)

The British launch also demonstrates **pre-release media exposure**. *The Observer* reported on 26 September 1999 that television and organized trading activity were already teaching the property before the games entered shops, echoing the media-first logic used in the United States. Contemporary UK reporting must still be paired with Sky One schedules, Nintendo UK advertisements, retail catalogs, and Wizards distribution material before a full national chronology is claimed. [*The Observer*, 26 Sept. 1999](https://www.theguardian.com/theobserver/1999/sep/26/life1.lifemagazine3)

Australia likewise had its own sequence and reception. Later franchise chronologies place the localized anime and Red/Blue in late 1998, but the stronger object-level record still needs network schedules, Nintendo Australia releases, and retail advertisements. By November 1999, an ABC radio report was documenting schoolyard card trading, supply constraints, and adult concern about the campaign. That contemporary record establishes saturation by that date without proving every earlier debut day. [ABC *PM*, 22 Nov. 1999](https://www.abc.net.au/pm/archive/articles/1999/11/22/s67800.htm)

**Research rule:** distinguish (1) trade presentation, (2) first local television broadcast, (3) game street date, (4) TCG availability, (5) first organized event, and (6) mass-cultural saturation for each country. “Pokémon launched in 1999” is a conclusion only after those separate clocks are aligned.

### Retail became its own institution

Pokémon Center Co. was established in April 1998 by Nintendo, Creatures, and Game Freak. A surviving launch flyer fixes the first Pokémon Center Tokyo's public opening in Nihonbashi to **25 April 1998 at 11:00**, while a later Pokémon Center executive interview independently gives the same date. In October 2000 the company was renamed The Pokémon Company and expanded from store operation to overall brand management. This legal/retail history is distinct from the fictional Pokémon Center and from later international stores. [The Pokémon Company corporate history](https://corporate.pokemon.co.jp/aboutus/history); [2018 Pokémon Center executive interview](https://www.famitsu.com/news/201803/27153897.html); [preserved opening flyer](https://trance-cell.com/db/ghahey98aha/)

The early object ecosystem was already broad. Bandai's Pokémon Kids soft-vinyl candy toys began in 1996; Takara Tomy's Monster Collection figures began in 1997; both converted the roster into numbered or repeatable physical collecting systems. Company histories disagree on some exact sub-line dates, so first-wave assortments will be established from packaging and trade catalogs. [Bandai Pokémon Kids history](https://www.bandai.co.jp/candy/pokemonkids/); [Takara Tomy Monster Collection history](https://www.takaratomy.co.jp/product_release/pdf/p100922.pdf)

### Everyday Japan: collection as a daily route

By 1997–98, Pokémon was no longer confined to game and hobby retail. Several licensed systems embedded collecting in repeated everyday actions:

- **Rail travel:** the 1997 JR East Pokémon Stamp Rally asked children to move through thirty Tokyo-area stations within a limited period. Ten stamps earned a trainer certificate; completion earned a folder with special Surfing Pikachu and Mew cards. Surviving reward sets corroborate the physical prizes, while later archival reporting reproduces a contemporary event account. The widely repeated “100,000 participants” figure currently rests on later business-history synthesis and should not be treated as a primary attendance count.
- **Packaged food:** Daiichi Pan dates the beginning of Pokémon Bread to 1 June 1998. The rotating *Deco Character Seal* inserts turned a perishable breakfast or snack purchase into a durable, serial collectible. This is a different social mechanism from a card pack: the wrapper was ordinary consumption; the sticker was the accumulating archive.
- **Air travel:** ANA's contemporary press release announced two domestically operated Pokémon Jets for 2 July 1998. It also specifies Pokémon paper cups, headrest covers, cabin-crew aprons, and an in-flight showing of *Pikachu's Vacation*. The aircraft skin was only the most visible layer of a coordinated cabin environment.
- **Specialist retail:** Pokémon Center Tokyo concentrated goods, brand presentation, and direct customer contact in a dedicated space, making the franchise itself—rather than a toy, food, or media category—the organizing principle of the store.

These systems explain the speed of cultural saturation better than a checklist of products. A child could encounter Pokémon while commuting, eating, traveling, reading, watching television, and shopping. For Pocket Archives, the strongest museum group would pair the glamorous object (aircraft photograph or completion cards) with the disposable infrastructure that made the experience ordinary: rally booklet, train ticket, bread wrapper, sticker, cup, headrest cover, shopping bag, receipt, and opening flyer.

**Source caution:** ANA's 1998 release incorrectly dates *Red/Green* to 28 February rather than Nintendo's documented 27 February date. This is a useful reminder that a primary source is authoritative about its own program, not automatically correct about every background fact it repeats. [ANA release, 3 June 1998](https://www.ana.co.jp/pr/980406-J/980603.html)

## Part X — Collecting history

### From children's circulation to condition hierarchy

Pokémon objects were collectible before they were financialized. The original systems deliberately encouraged completion, exchange, rarity, and display: version-exclusive game creatures, Mew lotteries, Carddass vending sequences, randomized TCG packs, numbered figures, stickers, and food premiums. Children handled these objects as playthings. That history explains why truly untouched early material is scarce, but it also means condition is part of an object's biography rather than the only measure of its museum value.

By 1999–2002, internet auctions and professional grading supplied a different infrastructure: standardized condition numbers, public populations, global price comparison, and registry competition. A 2002 PSA publication already lists 1999 Pokémon among Set Registry activity, showing that slab-centered completion was established within the franchise's first US generation. [PSA monthly publication, Sept. 2002](https://www.psacard.com/ezine/2002/9/3)

The early-2000s record is unusually fragile. Expired eBay listings, dead personal sites, lost forum attachments, and price-guide ephemera mean later collectors often reconstruct the period from memory. Pocket Archives should not turn those memories into a smooth price chart. The better evidence set is material: early PSA/Beckett holders with verifiable certification dates, printed population reports, complete price-guide issues, dated auction invoices, archived forum pages, convention photographs, and dealer catalogs.

### Four activities that should not be collapsed

| Activity | Primary aim | Typical evidence | Historical caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Collecting | Completion, attachment, play, display, or community | Binders, checklists, trades, letters, set registries | May involve price awareness without being primarily financial |
| Speculation | Buying because a near-term buyer may pay more | Rapid resale, unopened-product hoarding, launch-day secondary listings | Motive is difficult to prove from price movement alone |
| Investment | Capital allocation using expected long-term return and risk management | Portfolio language, cost basis, appraisal, insurance, exit strategy | Marketing often calls any purchase an “investment”; historian should require behavior or records |
| Archival/historical collecting | Preserving evidence of production, circulation, use, and context | Provenanced groups, wrappers, receipts, production files, oral histories | A worn common object may carry more information than a high-grade trophy |

These modes overlap. A child could collect and later sell; a museum can buy in a speculative market; an investor can fund preservation. The distinction concerns the dominant historical behavior, not a moral rank.

### Three later demand waves

1. **Mid-2010s nostalgia and Pokémon GO (2016):** the twentieth anniversary and mobile game's mass visibility brought lapsed players back to early cards and games. A 2016 PSA collecting feature records a specialist recalling PSA 10 first-edition Charizard sales around $600–700 in 2008 and $4,500–5,000 by 2016; this is anecdotal price memory but documents the scale of the pre-pandemic revaluation. [PSA Set Registry feature](https://www.psacard.com/articles/articleview/8911/psa-set-registry-collecting-1999-poke-mon-1st-edition-gaming-card-base-series-started-all)
2. **Pandemic/influencer acceleration (2020–21):** lockdown nostalgia, livestream openings, and mainstream auction promotion created rapid price increases and brought unopened product into high-value auction categories. Reuters contemporaneously reported the boom; Heritage's first dedicated trading-card event in July 2021 totaled $3.47 million, led by a $384,000 first-edition Base Set booster box. [Reuters report](https://www.investing.com/news/general/gotta-catch-em-all-pandemic-sends-prices-soaring-for-pokemon-cards-2426882); [Heritage results](https://comics.ha.com/heritage-auctions-press-releases-and-news/smashing-debut-heritage-auctions-first-trading-card-games-event-tops-3.4-million.s?releaseId=4254)
3. **Institutionalization and segmentation (2022–26):** population data, specialist auctions, authentication services, set registries, and international buying made “Pokémon collecting” several markets rather than one. PSA told Axios that Pokémon rose from 17% of cards it graded in 2018 to 43% in 2023. At the trophy apex, the sole PSA 10 Pikachu Illustrator moved from a Guinness-verified $5.275 million private transaction to a reported $16.5 million public auction in February 2026. These outliers document publicity and trophy-market concentration, not the value path of ordinary cards. [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2024/06/28/pokemon-card-psa-grading-value); [Guinness 2022 record](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/news/2022/7/the-5-million-pokemon-card-inside-logan-pauls-record-breaking-trade-708581); [Associated Press, 2026 auction](https://apnews.com/article/e4f17184466589e7313b58de734a06ad)

YouTube and livestreaming changed the object from a finished collectible into a performance. Pack opening, grading reveals, auction bidding, and “collection flex” videos made acquisition itself into content. During the pandemic boom, that attention interacted with restricted leisure, nostalgia, stimulus income for some buyers, and disrupted retail supply. No single factor explains the entire price movement; influencer visibility is one mechanism among several.

### Museum collecting versus trophy collecting

Pocket Archives should privilege explanatory density over grade. A worn 1996 Mew-offer *CoroCoro*, opened starter deck with rulebook, retailer order sheet, or child's annotated checklist may explain more history than a gem-mint Charizard. The acquisition roadmap therefore uses four tests:

- Does the object document a historical transition?
- Is its date and provenance independently verifiable?
- Can visitors understand what it did, not only what it costs?
- Is there a lower-cost original that tells the story better than a famous trophy?

Grading can authenticate a holder submission and describe condition, but it cannot repair missing provenance, prove a disputed production date merely by label, or substitute for historical research.

## Myths and misconceptions

| Claim | Earliest located source | Evidence for | Evidence against/qualification | Best current interpretation | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pokémon was invented because Tajiri collected insects. | TIME's 1999 interview is the earliest first-person account located so far, not necessarily the first ever published. | Tajiri directly described childhood collecting, observation, and disappearing habitats. | The same interview and development testimony also identify *Ultraman*, arcades, Game Freak's publishing network, game design, and the link cable. | A genuine influence that later journalism often compresses into a single-origin story. | High for influence; unresolved as a sole-cause claim |
| Every surviving Capsule Monsters image dates to 1990. | Modern grouping of disclosed proposal pages. | Several displayed proposal pages are explicitly dated 1990. | The booklet compiles material at visibly different stages; inclusion does not prove drawing date. | Date individual pages and objects, not the entire modern image corpus by association. | High |
| Sugimori designed all original 151 Pokémon. | Popular later summaries. | Sugimori served as art director and produced unified final illustrations. | In Nintendo's July 2000 staff interview, Sugimori explicitly described character ideation as a ten-person, cross-disciplinary effort. | Sugimori's visual unification role is broader than sole design authorship. | High; individual table pending |
| Pokémon took six continuous years to develop. | Retrospective shorthand based on 1990–1996 elapsed time. | The project did span roughly six calendar years. | Tajiri said in 1996 that active work occupied mainly the first year and final three years; other games deliberately interrupted it. | Six years elapsed; roughly four active years by Tajiri's early recollection. | High |
| Pokémon development nearly bankrupted Game Freak. | Popular retrospective summaries; the earliest exact wording has not been located. | Tajiri and Ishihara described real financial pressure and the need to complete paid projects to fund staff and continue. | No audited accounts, insolvency filing, or participant quotation located so far establishes literal near-bankruptcy. | Financial precarity is supported; “nearly bankrupt” remains an unverified dramatic compression. | Medium-high |
| Shigeru Miyamoto invented trading Pokémon. | Later summaries that collapse his producer role with Tajiri's link-cable concept. | Masuda credited Miyamoto with suggesting different cartridges, strengthening reciprocal exchange. | Tajiri and Sugimori's accounts attribute the original link-cable trading concept to Tajiri before the paired-version refinement. | Tajiri originated exchange as the central play idea; Miyamoto is credibly associated with the paired-version strategy. | High |
| Mew was merely an unauthorized programmer prank. | Later retellings of Morimoto's post-debug insertion. | Morimoto inserted the character after debugging freed cartridge space, and the feature presents an element of staff play. | Mew became an authorized promotional asset almost immediately; “unauthorized” and “never intended for release” require stronger evidence than the anecdote alone. | A staff-created secret whose internal approval status remains incompletely documented; its public distribution was unquestionably official. | Medium-high |
| Rhydon was definitively the first Pokémon designed. | Later synthesis of early documents and internal index order. | Rhydon appears prominently in early material and has a very low internal index. | Sugimori's phrasing identifies Rhydon, Clefairy, and Lapras among early designs, not a documented exact sequence; index order is not necessarily drawing order. | Rhydon is among the earliest surviving designs; “the first” remains probable, not proved. | Medium |
| Every leaked beta creature was intended for the released Generation I roster. | Modern leak and reconstruction culture. | Some discarded forms appear in genuine development sheets and later participant discussion. | A development corpus may include exercises, revisions, placeholders, alternate views, or ideas from different dates; appearance does not prove final-roster approval. | Describe the document state and evidence of use; avoid the stronger “cut from the final game” claim unless a source supports it. | High as a methodological correction |
| Topsun cards were printed in 1995 and are the first Pokémon cards. | The 1995 copyright line and later grading labels. | Cards carry a 1995 copyright. | A copyright year identifies the underlying IP, not necessarily manufacture; available product evidence points to 1997 and no 1995 Topsun retail record has been located. | Do not date Topsun to 1995 without contemporary distribution evidence. | Medium-high |
| Every Akabane-source prototype slab is an authentic 1995–96 object. | 2023–24 auction and grading labels. | Akabane was involved in the original card game and genuine development designs existed. | Printer tracking evidence prompted CGC to halt transactions and recall specified holders; provenance was not independently documented object by object. | Design history and physical-object authenticity must be evaluated separately; the affected market group is disputed. | High that blanket authentication is unsafe |
| The anime chose Pikachu because it was already the unquestioned mascot. | Retrospective simplification after Pikachu became globally dominant. | Pikachu won an internal design survey and was highlighted in the manual. | Yuyama and Kubo describe a television-specific need to avoid privileging any of the three selectable starters. | Pikachu had early internal appeal, but the anime choice helped create—not merely reflect—mascot status. | High |
| Satoru Iwata put Kanto into Gold and Silver by compressing the game. | Viral summaries of *Iwata Asks*. | Iwata wrote compression tools and provided major technical help on localization and Stadium work. | The cited interview does not state that his compression alone created room for Kanto. | His documented contribution is extraordinary without the embellished claim. | High |
| A high grade makes an object more historically important. | Modern price guides, registries, and auction presentation. | Condition materially affects scarcity and market price. | Historical significance also comes from use, inscriptions, completeness, context, and provenance; grading does not authenticate an undocumented story. | Grade and historical importance are separate axes. | High |
| Record trophy-card sales represent the Pokémon card market as a whole. | Headlines centered on Illustrator and Charizard. | Trophy transactions are real and influential signals. | Unique provenance, celebrity ownership, and tiny populations make them statistical outliers. | Use trophy sales to study collecting culture, not to value ordinary inventory. | High |

## One hundred objects that would tell Pokémon's history

The working acquisition database is maintained separately at [`acquisition-roadmap-100.csv`](./acquisition-roadmap-100.csv). It contains exactly one hundred candidate objects, each with a historical function, broad August 2026 planning range, difficulty score, likely search channels, verification priority, and source basis.

The list deliberately includes inexpensive paper, packaging, rules, and ordinary cards alongside rare development and production material. Price ranges are **budget bands, not appraisals**; they exclude buyer's premium, tax, shipping, conservation, currency movement, and provenance premium. No disputed prototype is recommended as an authentic period acquisition without item-level forensic and documentary proof.

## Bibliography and source notes

The full working bibliography is the connected [`source-ledger.csv`](./source-ledger.csv). It records title, author or interviewee, publication, original date, language, source class, location, sections supported, limitations, and review status. The table above is the compact primary-source reading room; the ledger also retains secondary scholarship, archival reconstructions, institutional histories, contemporary reporting, and market evidence needed to test conflicts.

This is deliberately not a flat “links” appendix. Sources are useful only in relation to claims: a corporate chronology is strong for its own launch date but selective about failure; participant memory supplies process but can drift over decades; a physical wrapper establishes wording and distribution but not necessarily the wider business decision; and a collector reconstruction can identify an unknown source without becoming the authority itself. Items marked **in review** are discovery leads or partially checked evidence, not equal substitutes for records marked **reviewed**.
